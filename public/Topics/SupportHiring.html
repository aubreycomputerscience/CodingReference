IT Hiring Committee: Who Gets the Job?

Teacher Guide

Purpose

Students have already spent time learning about careers and thinking about jobs they might want. This activity shifts away from another career-research assignment. Instead, students take the role of an employer and make a realistic hiring decision.

The larger idea is that getting an IT job is not just about knowing technology. Employers also consider communication, reliability, willingness to learn, customer service, experience, and certifications.

Scenario

You are part of the IT department for a school district.

School starts next week, and the district urgently needs to hire an entry-level Help Desk Technician.

The technician will:

  • Help teachers and staff when computers, printers, software, or accounts are not working.
  • Talk with users to figure out what the problem is.
  • Solve common technical problems.
  • Document what happened and what was done.
  • Pass difficult problems to more experienced IT staff.
  • Work with people who may be frustrated or know very little about computers.

Three candidates have applied.

Unfortunately, your department only has time to interview one candidate today.

Your job is to decide who gets the interview.

Lesson Flow

1. Opening Discussion — About 5 Minutes

Tell students:

Imagine that you run the school district’s IT department. School starts Monday, and you desperately need another Help Desk Technician. You have three applicants, but today you only have time to interview one of them.

Ask:

What would you look for in someone doing this job?

You can take answers verbally.

If students are quiet, use these prompts:

  • Does technical knowledge matter?
  • Does being good with people matter?
  • Does previous work experience matter?
  • Do certifications matter?
  • Would you rather hire somebody who already knows everything or somebody who learns quickly?
  • How important is reliability?

You do not need to establish the correct answer yet.

Then give students the candidate handout.

Teacher: Stop Here Before Revealing Part Two

Give students approximately 8–10 minutes to make their initial decision.

You can have students work alone, in groups of 2–4, or make an individual choice followed by group discussion.

Then ask for a quick show of hands:

  • Who picked Chris?
  • Who picked Jordan?
  • Who picked Taylor?

Do not announce a winner.

Instead, ask one student from each group to give one reason for choosing that candidate.

Then reveal the following new information.

Whole-Class Debrief

Now you can run the discussion without having to invent questions.

Question 1

Who would you interview now?

Take a quick vote.

Question 2

What does Chris have that the others don’t?

Expected student response:

Strongest demonstrated technical ability.

Question 3

What does Jordan have that Chris doesn’t?

Expected response:

Strong customer-service experience, reliability, communication experience, and evidence of dealing calmly with frustrated people.

Question 4

What does Taylor have that the others don’t?

Expected response:

Formal IT training/certification and some direct technology-support experience.

Taylor being bilingual may also be valuable for this particular workplace.

Question 5

Ask:

Which is easier for an employer to teach: how to replace a hard drive, or how to stay patient with an angry customer?

This is worth discussing rather than treating as an absolute fact.

The point you are trying to draw out is that technical skills can often be taught relatively quickly, while communication habits, reliability, judgment, and willingness to learn are also extremely important.

Question 6

Does having the most technical knowledge automatically make someone the best Help Desk Technician?

Answer:

No.

Help desk work sits between technical troubleshooting and customer service.

Someone can be technically excellent and still be poor at the job if users cannot communicate with them effectively.

Teacher Answer / Analysis

There deliberately is not one objectively correct candidate.

That is important to the exercise.

However, if you want a defensible professional answer, I would probably rank them:

1. Taylor — Strongest Overall Choice

Taylor is probably the safest interview choice based on the information provided.

Why:

Taylor already has entry-level IT training, has deliberately prepared for an IT career, has some actual support experience, appears reliable, and has references indicating that Taylor learns quickly.

Taylor’s weakness — being somewhat quiet — is something you can investigate during the interview.

The bilingual ability becomes an additional advantage because it directly helps serve this particular organization’s users.

2. Jordan — Very Strong Alternative

Jordan may actually turn into the best employee, despite having less technical experience.

Jordan already demonstrates things that are extremely important in help-desk work:

  • reliability
  • communication
  • patience
  • customer service
  • asking questions before acting
  • experience dealing with frustrated people

Basic IT troubleshooting can be taught.

Jordan also demonstrates initiative by studying for A+ independently.

If Jordan interviews well and demonstrates reasonable technical aptitude, hiring Jordan would be completely defensible.

3. Chris — Highest Technical Skill, Highest Hiring Risk

Chris may be the strongest technician technically.

For a different IT position, Chris might easily be the first choice.

But help-desk work requires constant interaction with users.

The supervisor’s comment about impatience is therefore particularly relevant.

The short job history also creates a reasonable question about retention.

The higher salary request isn’t inherently bad. Negotiating salary is normal. But from the employer’s perspective, it adds another consideration, especially when Chris already presents some risks for an entry-level customer-facing position.
