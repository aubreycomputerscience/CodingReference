Using Social Engineering to Teach Social Engineering - Mychilo S. Cline - Marketing groups seek to influence consumer behavior. Wikis attempt to stimulate and regulate discussion. Corporations attempt to influence work group dynamics. And online video games and virtual worlds attempt to build community and to promote user satisfaction. Social Engineering is used in the design of online communities, video games, wikis, work groups, and educational software. As we spend more and more time in the virtual world, the study of social engineering will continue to grow in importance. Although social engineering is often viewed as manipulative, social engineering may also be seen as a way to promote human happiness and well-being, to promote proper social functioning, or to enhance teamwork, productivity, or motivation. In fact, the design of any virtual environment – whether we are aware of it or not – is a social engineering project. Social engineering teaches us how to build virtual environments which optimize various aspects of human functioning or influence consumer behavior. Not only does the study of virtual environments teach us about how to manipulate each other, it creates public awareness of the tools which are used to manipulate us, helping us to navigate the virtual world and to think critically about the decisions we make. And this is particularly important in a democratic society, in which we participate in the political process, and in the marketplace, "buyer beware." In addition, the field of social engineering gives us insight in the field of education, in how to create an effective classroom or workplace environment or in how to develop effective classroom or workplace activities. And finally, the study of social engineering is instructive in the study of social science, illustrating important points in anthropology, sociology, and psychology, and teaching us about who we are as humans. In online environments, distribution of resources, reward and punishment, visibility, etc. may be used to influence individual behavior and group dynamics, promote internalization through personal investment, or to promote satisfaction and well-being. This may be accomplished through creating community, promoting goal attainment, or providing positive feedback. How best to teach these concepts or to illustrate them? Social engineering may best be taught through: Traditional instruction and illustration of basic concepts using everyday examples that we are already familiar with. Learning about group dynamics and social engineering through participation in classroom activities, such as playing cards. Analyzing how social engineering techniques are used within the classroom, office, church, and community, or in advertising or online. And by having students explore and experiment with the design of classroom activities or virtual environments which actively employ social engineering to promote various outcomes. One approach to teaching social engineering is to analyze and reflect upon how social engineering is used in daily life and activity. How does the proximity and visibility of your teacher, employer, parent, or authority figure change your behavior? In online environments, who is allowed to talk and when? How much data is transmitted and how much delay is there? What degree of social visibility is there? How do your teachers use social engineering techniques in the classroom? How does your favorite video game uses the distribution of resources and people to influence game play and create a rewarding experience? Nevertheless, without guidance and instruction, student work tends to be superficial, highlighting the need for more traditional instruction. Given twenty years of reflection, students are unlikely to learn calculus. But if they are given instruction, it is a different matter altogether. Traditional instruction, lecture, illustration, and discussion may be used to teach students about social engineering. Lecture, for one, is a particularly efficient way of transmitting information. But without entertaining illustrations and catchy sound-bites, retention is likely to be limited. Fortunately, in the case of social engineering, there is no shortage of entertaining material. ". . . Everything you need to know about influencing group dynamics you can learn from picking up on a girl at a bar. You can't pick up on a girl in front of her father. You always need a wingman . . . ." The shortcomings of lecture are well-known, nevertheless it is often better to spend fifteen minutes reviewing the life-cycle of a frog, than to spend months watching it grow from a tadpole. And this also applies to social engineering. Yet, there is an important role for games and activities and experimentation. It is through using, experimenting with, and struggling with the implications, that students gain true mastery of the material and learn to apply it in new situations. We may evaluate the effectiveness of activities in terms of cost benefit: Time requirement. (Is it worthwhile?) Risk. Learning. Contribution to the Learning Environment. (We want a classroom that is rewarding, engaging, and fun.) A problem with all classroom activities are that they are time consuming. It takes time to learn the game and to play it. (Alternatively, computer science students may experiment with the design of virtual communities, an even more time consuming task - but this is beyond the scope of our discussion.) As students participate in games and activities - or in the design of virtual environments - different students will have different experiences, suggesting the need for group discussion and the sharing of experiences, which although profitable, may be time consuming. An additional problem is that games are not without risk. It is important for students to explore how social engineering may lead to both pleasant and unpleasant consequences, nevertheless, some supervision may be required. Some games may result in hurt feelings, undermine the learning environment, or create trouble with parents and administrators. Asking students to evaluate how other teachers use social engineering techniques in the classroom is an excellent assignment, but one that may get you into trouble. And it is important to remember that a well taught course will result in surprises, and it is thus important to have administrators who are "on board." Although games and activities present risks, they also offer an invaluable learning experience. Rich Man-Poor Man (also called "asshole") is one of several games that may be used to illustrate important concepts in social engineering. Rich Man-Poor Man (a.k.a. "asshole") The Game: Students sit around a table. At the head of the table is the king. To his left is the queen. Then there are the towns people. And finally, the last two players are the servant and the slave. The king deals, passing all the cards out, starting with the slave and working backwards. The slave is required to give his two best cards to the king, in exchange for two bad ones. And the servant is required to give his best card to the queen, in exchange for a bad one. Game Play: The goal of the game is to get rid of all your cards,. The 1st person to get rid of all their cards get to be the king, the second, the queen, and so on and so forth. At the end of the game, everyone stands up and moves to the appropriate location. The king plays first. The queen next. And around the table. If you wish to play, you must play a higher card. The highest card wins the round. After winning the round, that person is able to lead any card they desire, thus starting the next round. Ace is high, a pair beats a single card, three of a kind beat a pair, and a 2 is trump. Changing Game-Play: In order to experiment with social engineering, the King is allowed to create a rule, such as making the servant and slave wear silly hats, say something silly, every time they play a card, sit on the floor, etc. Analysis: Although it took students a long time to learn the game (it maybe a better idea to assign it as homework), the game was worthwhile and students gained considerable insight. Some of the responses from students include: Without cost, games are no fun. Goal attainment leads to a sense of accomplishment. Repeated failure to advance one's position may lead to feelings of being disenfranchised or held-down. Competition, reward and punishment, and personal embarrassment create motivation, but may also lead to anger, resentment, or withdrawal. Cost creates investment. Investment fosters motivation. Motivation results in further investment. Investment may promote a sense of community. Behavior may be shaped through reward and punishment. Short-term goals with immediate feedback are particularly rewarding. Different students, of course, learned different things. As a result, it is necessary for students to share their conclusions in group discussion. Once again, there is risk involved. One of my favorite students, for instance, lost the game and was asked by the king to sit on the floor. Unfortunately, she was not tall enough to see the cards on the table when sitting on the floor, leading to considerable embarrassment. As a teacher, you must ask, should I intervene? Or is this part of a valuable lesson. In this case, I choose not to intervene. The student later reflected that embarrassment lead to some resentment, but by in large, motivated her to learn the game and to better her peers. Or in the language of social engineering, reward and punishment shaped her behavior, teaching her to play more effectively - a valuable lesson in social engineering. When teaching social engineering, unfortunately, risk and reward walk hand-in-hand. In contrast with Rich Man-Poor Man, Texas Hold-Em, while no doubt illustrating important concepts, did not provide a lot of "bang for the buck," and reflections upon what we know about group behavior and reward and punishment tells us why. Texas Hold-Em has a difficult learning curve, and does not provide the same level of competition, provide as effective reward and punishment (at least when playing for pennies), nor does it create and maintain hierarchy, important in discussing group dynamics. One of the best ways of studying social engineering is by using social engineering in the classroom. That is, using social engineering on the students and having them analyze and reflect. Brad Arnold, for instance, divided his class into teams - Net, Bit, Byte, & Mouse - and applied reward and punishment to the class, as a whole, as they competed for the "gold cup" (reminding one of Harry Potter). This approach was an effective way to promote participation and group work, and provided an opportunity for students to reflect on the use of social engineering and to see how it is "used on them" in daily life, helping them to think more critically. In one of my classes, we played a spiced-up version of Shoots and Ladders, with the inclusion of black holes, magic spells, and special powers, and tied it in with homework and class participation. "Do good work, you get to roll the dice." Students greatly enjoyed the game. "Trap someone on a desert island. Stick two people together with bubble gum. Don't forget about the Global Exchange Program." On one hand, the game changed student attitudes about turning in homework - "please, please collect our homework" - and it created a fun classroom environment, promoting participation and personal investment. The drawbacks of the game were that it wasted a lot of classroom time, did not provide a great deal of insight into group dynamics, and took away from the serious of the course. Thus, Shoots and Ladders does not have a lot to recommend itself, although it could be improved by altering game play so that it does not take up so much class time, making it more worthwhile. Finally, simulations, such as political simulations, may provide more in depth treatment of group formation and maintenance, but may have significant risk. Running the Stanford Prison Experiment in your classroom is probably not a good idea. Watching a video of the Stanley Milgram experiment might be a better idea. The problem with experimenting with group formation and maintenance is that it can easily get out of hand - and in a social environment, you never know for sure exactly what is going to happen. Experiments in group formation and maintenance are perhaps best reserved for experienced teachers or online communities, and may require extensive scaffolding and careful supervision. In conclusion, social engineering is best taught through a combination of traditional instruction, analysis of real-world situations, and by games and activities. In addition, advanced students may experiment with the design of online communities, where they are able to modulate visibility, delay, and other important social engineering variables. Traditional lessons and instruction should emphasis entertaining illustrations and memorable sound bites - "You can't pick up on a girl in font of her dad." Analysis and discussion should be used to apply these lessons to real life scenarios. And games and activities (or the design of virtual communities) should allow students to experiences and to experiment with social engineering for themselves. In this process, different forms of instruction should be used in a cyclical manner and different forms of instruction should reinforce one-another. Without mutual reinforcement, students may never learn to truly gain mastery over the social engineering curriculum; yet with effective instruction, students will begin to view education, marketing, education, and group and interpersonal interaction, in a critical and sophisticated manner, and may begin to apply it in other fields of academic inquiry. In a similar manner, social engineering may help educators view classroom experience in a more sophisticated and critical manner. Social engineering may be viewed as a toolset, helping us to understand and control interpersonal interaction patterns, to structure classroom activities, and to develop teamwork, participation, or motivation. The study of massive, online, role-playing games, in particular, may give us insight into this process.   The physical environment may also be used to influence human behavior. Changes in the physical environment (e.g. geography, climate, etc.) may place constraints on human behavior and promote specific behaviors or tendencies. Anthropologists have noted how different societies have developed and adapted within the constraints of the natural environment. In particular, anthropologists believe that how a group “makes a living” (“subsistence systems”) affects cultural and behavioral patterns. For instance, hunters and gatherers make a living by moving from place to place in search of food. 2 The size of these groups is generally limited by the quantity of available food (in anthropology, this is called “carrying capacity”) to between 15 and 25 people. Small group interaction, combined with a migratory lifestyle, promote particular cultural features. Individuals, for instance, are expected to do their share of the work, and not to act overly self-important.3 In addition, it is important to note that hunter and gatherers are unable to save work or resources for future use. For instance, it is not possible to stockpile food because it will spoil and cannot be carried in a mobile lifestyle. In contrast “civilization” is characterized by the organization of production and labor, specialization and social stratification, long-term planning and the accumulation of knowledge. In a similar manner, the design of an urban environment may influence human cultural and behavior patterns. This is demonstrated, to some extent, by city planning. In 1938, a city planner named Louis Boulonnois undertook one of the first 137 VIRTUAL GENETICS social engineering projects.4 Boulonnois believed that urban planning could be used to build a better world. Rabinow explains that: [Boulonnois] hoped to create New Men freed, purified, and liberated to pursue new forms of sociality which would inevitably arise from correctly designed spaces and forms. Science, particularly social science, would define humanity’s needs, and technical planners would meet them.5 Boulonnois hoped to use urban planning to alter behavior patterns. For example, he attempted to alter family size and provided incentives for individuals to have steady jobs. In order to reduce family size, he reduced the size of the average house. In order to encourage individuals to have steady jobs, he created housing with modern amenities, such as electricity, and monthly utility bills. Thus, in order to enjoy the modern amenities of life, it was necessary to have a regular job. In this way, the distribution of space, resources, and desirable goods may be used in influence and constrain human behavior. In a similar manner, it is possible to alter the distribution of resources in a video game to alter large scale behavior patterns. Large multi-player video games, such as EverQuest, Ultima Online, and Asheron’s Call mirror the real world in many ways. They have their own cultures, value-systems, economies, politics, etc. It is possible to influence the formation of economic systems within massive multi-player online role playing games, for instance, by altering the distribution of resources and game mechanics. Economics may be influenced by changing the distribution of resources, altering production, or limiting the number of players. This is particularly evident in games like Civilization and StarCraft in which one builds a civilization or strike force. This is also seen in games, such as EverQuest. Nicholas Yee explains that: . . . we can look at how game mechanics influence community-wide behaviors or phenomena. For example, it is probably fairly obvious that the game mechanics of an MMORPG affect the economy that develops within the game. If there are limited ways for the currency to leave the player market (through NPC vendors, death 138 INFLUENCING BEHAVIOR penalty, etc.), then inflation will eventually overtake the economy and be difficult to control.6 Within a virtual environment, a change in economics may influence other cultural features, such as value-systems (e.g. charity, work ethic) and practices (e.g. stealing, killing, pillaging). In this way, changes in game mechanics may be used to influence the formation and maintenance of cultural patterns, found within MMORPGs. In addition, the physical environment may be designed to influence mood. The potential influence of the physical environment on mood is exemplified by ooohhhs and ahhhs at the Grand Canyon; high suicide rates in Sweden during the long winter (as a result of limited daylight hours); the cheerfulness of a children’s playroom (filled with bright colors, sunshine, etc.); and irritability in 120 degree weather. In virtual environments, it will be possible to influence mood by altering the physical surroundings, light, and temperature. Virtual Genetics In the real world, genetic engineering may be used to make bones stronger or to make you jump higher. In the virtual world, virtual genetics may be used to change the virtual body. That is, in the virtual world it is possible to make you jump higher, to alter physical appearance, or to add wings and a tail, to alter your ‚virtual genetics. ‛ In this way, it is possible to talk about virtual genetics and about making changes to the virtual body. In addition, virtual genetics may be used to influence interpersonal communication, human cognition, personality and adjustment. And this is where it gets interesting. Communication technologies may change the quantity, intensity, and type of information that is transmitted between individuals. And may be used to alter the message, FOCUS ATTENTION, alter sensory threshold / ease of arousal, etc. For example, if you play with an environment so it sounds like everyone is YELLING, it is likely to create discomfort and may result in withdrawal. Withdrawal, in turn, may influence personality and adjustment. In this way, it is possible to use virtual environments, not only to alter the physical body, but to alter temperament and personality. Virtual Genetics: Increasing visual depth perception in virtual environments Each of us has two eyes, which look at the world from a slightly different perspective. If you close one of your eyes, you will see a flat, nearly two dimensional image, although your brain is still able to pick out some depth cues. But if you open both eyes, the brain is able to is able to create a true 3-dimensional image, combining the two dimensional images from each of our eyes into a single 3-dimensional image. Yet, there are limitations. At infinity, our 3-dimensional perspective flattens and we loose depth perception. The two images, one from each eye, are nearly identical and the brain does not have enough information to distinguish how far an object is away; it does not have the depth cues it needs to provide a 3- dimensional perspective. This is merely a function of biology, ‚How far apart from each other are your eyes?‛ If it were possible, however, to move your eyes further apart from one another, depth perception would increase. The further apart the images are, the more accurately the brain can sketch a 3-D model of the external world. This may be simulated easily enough, as demonstrated by neuroscientist and Professor Dr. Paul Churchland (who’s work provided the inspiration for this short article). And, this is precisely what we can do in 3-dimensional virtual displays. In a virtual display, different images are displayed to each eye creating depth perception. If you have ever watched a 3-D movie, you have experienced this for yourself. And by making minor alterations to the images, just moving it over a little bit (increasing the distance between your eyes), it is possible to increase depth perception – increasing depth perception has become a trivial matter.
