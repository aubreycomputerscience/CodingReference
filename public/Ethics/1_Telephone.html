The Telephone

Power, Madness, and Immortality: The Future of Virtual Reality (2005), Chapter 3

Introduction

With the introduction of the telephone came changes in social and economic life. In business, the telephone changed patterns of commerce and trade, increased the speed of business, altered the organization of corporations, and enabled companies to sell new products and services. In addition, with the rise of electrical technologies, such as the telephone, the telegraph, and the light bulb, there was an emergence of a new technical class, the electrical engineer. With the electrical engineer came trade journals filled with the utopian rhetoric of science and progress, filled with hopes of building a better world, as well as with fears of the breakdown of social structures and the erosion of moral codes.

In domestic life, the telephone changed the way social contact was ritualized. The telephone made it possible to meet friends and relatives, as well as the local shopkeeper, without leaving the house or sending a letter. Nevertheless, it was the source of some confusion, as described in popular literature and trade journals of the time. People were not used to using the telephone and were not always ready for new situations that they suddenly found themselves in. Society had to come to terms with how to interpret these new situations. It was necessary for society to develop standards and rules to regulate behavior.

This chapter will look at the history and use of the telephone, discuss what is different about talking on the telephone, and investigate the social impact of the telephone. It will conclude that the telephone resulted in new forms of social contact, new ways of doing business, the creation of a technical class, the spread of utopian and dystopian literature, and a minor renegotiation of social structure and norms.

A Brief History of the Telephone

Before the invention of the telephone, a wide network of telegraphs, pneumatic tubes, and messengers were used to transmit information. It was a fairly advanced and effective system, which reached almost every corner of the ‘civilized world.’ The use of the telegraph was described by a British writer:

The American telegraph . . . is employed in transmitting messages to and from bankers, merchants, members of Congress, officers of government, brokers, and police officers; parties who by agreement have to meet each other at the two stations, or have been sent for by one of the parties; items of news, election returns, announcements of deaths, inquiries respecting the health of families and individuals, daily proceedings of the Senate and the House of Representatives, orders for goods, inquiries respecting the sailing of vessels, proceedings of cases in various courts, summoning of witnesses, messages for express trains, invitations, the receipt of money at one station and its payment at another; for persons requesting the transmission of funds from debtors, consultation of physicians, and messages of every character usually sent by the mail. The confidence in the efficiency of telegraphic communication has now become so complete, that the most important commercial transactions daily transpire by its means between correspondents several hundred miles apart.1

The telephone, to some extent, may be seen as an extension of the telegraph. Early telegraphs, called optical telegraphs, were little more than messages sent from one flag waving station to another. With the invention of the electrical telegraph, flags were replaced by electrical signals. The invention of the telephone, the addition of a microphone and speaker at each end, would appear to be the next logical step. Nevertheless, the story of the development of the telephone is somewhat haphazard and comical.

The telephone was first developed by Alexander Graham Bell in 1876.2 Bell speculated that:

It is conceivable that cables of telephone wires could be laid underground, or suspended overhead, communicating by branch wires with private dwellings, country houses, shops, manufactories, etc., etc., uniting them through the main cable with a central office where the wire could be connected as desired, establishing direct communication between any two places in the city. Such a plan as this, though impracticable at the present moment, will, I firmly believe, be the outcome of the introduction of the telephone to the public. Not only so, but I believe in the future wires will unite the head offices of the Telephone Company in different cities, and a man in one part of the country may communicate by word of mouth with another in a different place.3

Like many inventors before him, Bell was plagued by technological difficulties and financial problems. Bell struggled to raise money, convince skeptics, and sell his invention. In fact, the British Post Office (which owned the British telegraph) turned down the opportunity to buy the patent, believing the telegraph to be a superior technology.4 One of Bell’s problems was that no one was quite sure what the telephone should be used for. This uncertainty is seen in early advertisements for the telephone, which focus on how to use a telephone, but are strangely quiet about what one might use it for.5

Over time, the telephone slowly evolved. Originally, the telephone was only one-directional. It was used, like a radio, to transmit “music, drama, and news.”6 With the advent of the two-directional telephone, broadcasting was abandoned until the invention of the radio, a half century later.7 The telephone, according to a special 1907 report in Telephone, was next used in business, as an aid to commerce (like the telegraph).8 Although the public initially did not distinguish between the telegraph and the telephone, the telephone supplemented and eventually replaced the intercontinental telegraph network because it was less expensive and easier to use.9

As the price of the telephone dropped, it began to find its way into the middle class American home. Initially, it was used only for business and emergencies, but before long it became used primarily, not for business, but for social interaction.10 In a period of over fifty years, the telephone spread to the homes of America. During this time, the technology continued to improve and evolve. In particular, the party line was replaced by the private line. On the party line, eavesdropping had been a source of entertainment and education. With the advent of the private line, however, interaction over the telephone became more intimate.11

What Is Different About the Telephone

Speaking to someone on the telephone is in many ways like speaking to someone in the dark. It is not possible to see many of the traditional cues, such as facial expression, that we use to help communicate. In other words, non-verbal cues are not available in telephone conversation. Some types of non-verbal communication include: facial expression, eye movement, posture, gestures, touch, vocal tone, physical distance, clothing, looks, physical setting, and movement.12 These cues provide information about how one “feels” about what is being said. In addition, they may provide social information about one’s economic level, education, trustworthiness, social position, sophistication, success, character, background, etc.

Although research suggests that the absence of visual cues does not greatly affect conversation,13 there clearly are some important differences between face-to-face and telephone interaction. Missing cues affect the quality of communication, as well as one’s perception of privacy. One may note that it is much easier to get into fights over the telephone, or to “break up” over the telephone. One might also observe that it is possible for individuals, such as market researchers, to ask more personal and embarrassing questions over the phone.14 In telephone counselling, Lester explains, it has been found that the telephone creates anonymity, reduced dependency, accessibility, unreality, immediacy.15 In addition, Goddard reports that telephone contact, on average, is shorter and less likely to be planned. Moreover, one is likely to talk about fewer things, and less likely to meet someone new.16

Although the telephone enables long distance communication, most telephone conversations are with people easily within visiting distance. The telephone is used primarily to reinforce existing relationships.17 One of the reasons for this is that when conflict arises on the telephone, it is difficult to “make-up” on the telephone. Thorngren explains that the viability of contact with “self-repairing capacity” is difficult to achieve over long distances.18 In other words, it is difficult to have a sustained relationship exclusively over the telephone because it is difficult to forgive someone over the telephone.

A New Form of Social Contact

The telephone was a new form of social contact, which ritualized social contact in new ways. Not only is it used to maintain personal relationships, it is used to help take care of daily business. According to psychologists, the purpose of social contact is belonging, influence, affection, and cooperation.19 The telephone helps to facilitate the social contact used to fulfill these needs. In her ethnography, Lana Rakow notes that the telephone is important in creating a sense of family and community, explaining that:

Women in Prospect [a small mid-western town] use the telephone, then, for maintaining relationships with family and friends, for accomplishing the ongoing structure of the community, for giving and receiving care and emotional support, and for doing the business work that enters the private sphere.20

In addition, Kingsbury reports that early advertisements for the telephone emphasized the telephone’s ability to help with daily chores, “In domestic life, the telephone can put one in instant contact with the grocer, butcher, baker.”21 The early telephone, in particular, was also a source of entertainment. The now defunct party line (the original reality T.V.) was a source of entertainment for the old and provided an education to the adolescent.22

The telephone changed one’s sphere of influence and power. Furthermore, it enabled the creation of a new type of social network, a network available to the general public. Cherry argues that the telephone exchange, for the first time in history, offered a choice of social contacts, on demand, even between strangers, without ceremony, introduction, or credentials.23 Although the extent to which this is true is a matter of some debate, it is true in a very important sense. This issue will be revisited later in the chapter.

New Ways of Doing Business

The telephone and the automobile together (like the telegraph and the train before them) had a profound impact on business organization and activity. The speed of transportation and communication resulted in new patterns of commerce and trade,24 changed the organization of companies, enabled the creation of new products and services, and changed the city’s skyline.

The skyscraper best exemplifies the impact of the telephone on business. In order to do business, it was no longer necessary to have physical proximity. Thus, the telephone allowed a business to centralize operations to a single inexpensive location and to do business elsewhere. In addition, the telephone was helpful in the construction of skyscrapers because it allowed a supervisor to direct his workers on the scaffolding from the ground.25 More importantly, it allowed one to transmit information to someone on a different floor without having to resort to sending messenger boys helter-skelter up and down the elevators.26 John Carty illustrates this point:

It may sound ridiculous to say that Bell and his successors were the fathers of modern commercial architecture – of the skyscraper. But wait a minute. Take the Singer Building, the Flatiron, the Broad Exchange, the Trinity, or any of the giant office buildings. How many messengers do you suppose would go in and out of those buildings everyday? Suppose there was no telephone and every message had to be carried by a personal messenger. How much room do you think the necessary elevators would leave for offices? Such structure would be an economic impossibility.27

The development of the skyscraper exemplifies how the telephone changed the business world. The telephone allowed one to “work at a distance” and allowed companies to reorganize to create cost savings and new efficiencies.

The telephone may be viewed as a predecessor to “information technology” (a term used in business to describe automated systems used to manipulate large quantities of data or to create efficiencies), in that it was used to collect and organize information, improve processes, and restructure companies. In this way, the telephone may be seen as an organizational tool used in the organization and pooling of labor and resources: the movement of objects, people, and ideas. In other words, the telephone made it easier to better coordinate the activity of a large number of people, and increased the speed of many business processes.

The telephone became important in industrial and manufacturing activities.28 It led to the development of new social organizations,29 and new products and services, such as the telephone book, the suicide prevention hotline, and phone sex. In fact, it was possible to buy groceries over the telephone.30 (Although for some reason, in spite of convenience and time savings, this did not seem to catch on.) More recently, the decrease in long distance telephone rates and the low cost of foreign labor has resulted in the outsourcing of customer support service centers to other countries, such as India.

In conclusion, the telephone changed the speed and organization of business. In addition, it changed daily activities, leaving businessmen at their desks and replacing face-to-face communication with telephone conversation, often between individuals who had never met. With the adoption of the telephone, there was also further specialization, because it was no longer necessary to be a jack of all trades. Finally, the telephone played a vital role in the transformation of the American economy into a modern industrial economy, and then to a post-industrial service economy.

The Formation of a New Class of Professionals

With the discovery of electricity and the ensuing development of electrical technologies (the telegraph, the telephone, and the light bulb), there was a rise of a new technical class, the electrical professional.31 The rise of the electrical professional is in many ways analogous to the rise of the I.T. professional (the web designer, the software engineer, the techie). Like I.T. professionals, electrical professionals had authority, affluence, and power. They were instrumental in the “construction of knowledge” and looked down upon people who did not understand the technology.

The Spread of Utopian Rhetoric

With the rise of a technical class came the utopian rhetoric of science and progress. The myths of novelty, change, and progress were prevalent in popular literature and electrical journals of the time.32 (In 1890, Electricity reported that there were between 30 and 40 electrical periodicals in Britain alone.)33

Carolyn Marvin describes:

Much of the literature on electricity in the late nineteenth century can be read as the wishful template of a world that electrical professionals believed they would create, given the opportunity . . . .

For them [the electricians], electricity was the transformative agent of social possibility. Through their power over it, it would be a creator of social miracles.34

A quote from Electrical World, in 1884, illustrates this point.

We take it that everything [anything, such as telephone] which can knit a community together and which can cause a rapid interchange of sentiment and ideas, annihilate distance, isolation and prejudice, is of [will promote] the greatest happiness to the greatest number.35

But, there were also concerns about the new technology. Electrical professionals, according to Marvin, were particularly concerned about “the impact of new media on the family, class, community and gender relations.”36 Marvin explains that the:

. . . world that looked the most comfortable to them was less the egalitarian one they professed to desire in their more self-conscious public moments than one in which the stability of familiar social and class structures would be preserved . . . .37

In addition, there were concerns about human well-being, in a futuristic society, as exemplified by a journalistic comment in Electrician, in 1892:

. . . we are getting perilously near the ideal of the modern Utopian when life is to consist of sitting in arm chairs and pressing a button. It is not a desirable prospect; we shall have no money, no ambition, no youth, no desires, no individuality, no names and nothing wise about us.38

The Renegotiation of Social Norms

The telephone was, initially, often a cause of confusion and concern. “The invention of new machinery, devices, and processes,” reflected Telephony in 1905, “is continually bringing up new questions of law, puzzling judges, lawyers, and laymen.”39 People had to learn how to act in new situations; and different individuals and groups tried to use the telephone in their own self-interest, whether a parent, a lusty young daughter, a police officer, or a criminal. Difficulties adapting to the telephone were described in the popular literature, stories of mistakes, love, invasion, and misunderstanding.40

Marvin retells a number of humorous anecdotes found in the electrical literature: a woman’s embarrassment when answering the phone not properly dressed,41 a prominent newspaper editor warning his reader about the dangers of contracting contagious diseases over the telephone,42 and about a man who was reprimanded for not keeping to his own business, after using the telephone to report a fire to the local fire brigade.43 These stories, whether true or not, suggest that society did not fully understand the technology, and furthermore, that society was in a process of negotiating how the telephone should be used and understood.

Marvin suggests that the introduction of the telephone challenged many social institutions and traditions, including class, family, and courting behavior. For instance, the telephone made it difficult to control a daughter’s access to an inappropriate suitor:

. . . some people were suddenly too close and others much too far away . . . . new encounters clashed with old ways of determining truth and reliability, and with old notions about the world and one’s place in it: about the relation of men and women, rich and poor . . . .44

and:

. . . electrical communication made families, courtships, class identities, and other arenas of interaction suddenly strange, with consequences that were tirelessly spun out in electrical literature.45

In addition, Marvin argues that the introduction of new technologies, such as the telephone, is a drama in which groups perpetually negotiate power, authority, representation, and knowledge:46

. . . the introduction of new media is a special historical occasion when patterns anchored in older media that have provided the stable currency of social exchange are re-examined, challenged, and defended . . . the early history of the electric media is less the evolution of technical efficiencies in communication than a series of arenas for negotiating issues critical to the conduct of social life; among them, who is inside and outside, who may speak, who may not, and who has authority and may be believed.47

Anthropologists, such as Rakow, however, are likely to disagree, suggesting that social hierarchy and practices leave little room for negotiation. In addition, anecdotal reports from the 1930s and 1940s de-emphasize the importance of the telephone in daily life and in the “process of negotiation,” emphasizing the slow and gradual evolution of the telephone and its use.

Yet, it was clearly necessary for society to agree upon new practices, to define acceptable behavior (expectations, privacy, and law), and to formalize how a telephone conversation should progress. In this age, it is hard to imagine how strange it was to have a telephone suddenly ring without warning, to interrupt, to come into one’s home uninvited and unannounced. Ronell describes how, “You don’t know who’s calling or what you are going to be called upon to do, and still, you are lending your ear, giving something up, receiving an order.”48 Thus, society had to evolve and adjust to deal with new situations and eventualities, to develop new practices and rules of social contact and behavior (such as not calling during dinner). Thus, one might conclude that, although this process of negotiation was likely to be relatively gradual and subtle, and furthermore, to have been greatly constrained or inhibited by social practices, the introduction of the telephone created some “wiggle room” for the negotiation of social practices and norms.

Popular Opinion of the Telephone

Brooks reports that just before and just after the invention of the telephone, it was looked on with suspicion and viewed as a moral threat.49 Before long, however, the apprehension had faded. The telephone became a status symbol and the rural female telephone operator became a folk hero.

Not everyone was excited about the telephone and its potential to act as an agent of social change, however. The French, in particular, did not welcome the telephone. Attali and Stourdze argue that the telephone was rejected because it threatened the social hierarchy. They explain that French society was built on hierarchy – one-way monologue, and the telephone challenged this one-way monologue, promoting “dialogue,” “reciprocity,” equality, and access.50 Thus, the French found the telephone to be more like a two-way intercom, than the summoning bell that they had hoped for.51 In addition, Attali and Stourdze report that the official doctrine of the French government was that the transmission and dispersal of news, brought by new technologies such as the telephone, was akin to “poison” and “explosives,” “insurrection” and “disorder.”52

As a result of their suspicion of the telephone, the French were slow to adopt the technology. At the turn of the twentieth century, there were roughly the same number of telephones in New York’s top 100 largest hotels, as in all of France.53 Even by 1968, only 60% of French households were equipped with telephones.54 Moreover, the telephone hardly worked. Pierce reports that the situation was so bad that people did not bother to make arrangements or chat on the telephone.55

Four Areas of Confusion

The introduction of the telephone created four main areas of confusion: maintaining boundaries, adjusting to the rapid spread of information, learning to avoid deception, and interpreting new social roles, such as the telephone girl. Maintaining boundaries was important to the rich and powerful, as well as within the home and family. Marvin explains, “Class and family boundaries could be maintained only so long as inadmissible outsiders were effectively screened.”56 Marvin asks, quoting Telephony:

“. . . The doors may be barred and a rejected suitor kept out, but how is the telephone to be guarded?” New forms of communication created unprecedented opportunities not only for courting and infidelity, but for romancing unacceptable persons outside one’s own class, and even one’s own race . . . .57

Although the telephone challenged class and family boundaries, social practice continued to support, and for the most part, preserve the existing social structure. In her ethnography, Rakow describes her observations of a small, American, mid-western town, observing that social practice generally controlled access to the telephone.

Though the telephone has the technical capacity to level social hierarchies, . . . technical possibility has not translated into social practice.58

. . . the telephone does not exist separately from social practices that give it shape and function, these social practices served to maintain hierarchy when challenged by a new technology.59

In addition, the courts tended to back prevailing norms and institutions, as demonstrated by an Ohio court, which ruled:

The telephone reaches into many family circles. . . . All communications should be in proper language. Moreover, in many cases the operators in the exchanges are refined ladies, and even beyond this, all operators should be protected from insult.60

The rapid spread of information also created a number of humorous stories. Marvin retells a story of failed romance, from Electrical World, which exemplifies how the speed of communications could be problematic.

During the summer of 1886 a “nice young man” from the city met “one of the rustic beauties of the place” and fell in love. They corresponded, and she invited him to visit. One day a telegram appeared with news of his impending arrival. “Somehow-nobody ever will know just how-fifteen minutes after the message clicked into the office every person in the town knew that young Blake was coming to see Miss Trevette. Every young lady of the town made up her mind to catch a glimpse of this rash young man who sent telegrams, and every man determined to be there to see that everything went smoothly.” When young Blake alighted from his carriage, full of the foreboding that marked him as a proper suitor, an audience of 499 villagers had gathered to watch . . . and followed him to the young lady’s house. Panicked by the approaching procession, Miss Trevette sent word of her absence, halting the romance at a blow.61

The speed of communications had already been disruptive in the time of the telegraph, making it impossible for governments to hide what was going on in war-time, and making diplomacy difficult.62 (In fact, according to a French historian, the hasty dispatch of diplomatic messages resulted in the Franco-Prussian War of 1870-71.)63 The problems created by the speed and ease of telegraphic communication, were multiplied many times over, with the ubiquity of the telephone.

The rapid spread of information, and the lack of privacy on the party line, provided new opportunities for burglars and criminals. The Baird Manufacturing Company, a home security company, reported that police had discovered that many robberies had been “planned and executed in the city from information obtained by ‘listening in’ on party lines.”64 On the other hand, the telephone was also used by women to notify the police about suspicious characters, and by the police to help apprehend suspects.

The telephone also provided new opportunities for identity theft and deception (being phoney). In 1888, the Chicago Herald ran a reportedly factual story of deception and intrigue.

A millionaire speculator at the Chicago Board of Trade had a phone line installed between his office and his home, one of the first such lines in the city. One day he answered a call from a man who claimed that he had bound and gagged the man’s wife and servants and set oily rags around the house. The businessman was instructed to give twenty thousand dollars from an office safe to a confederate waiting at that moment outside his office – or else. The ransom delivered, he rushed home to discover his wife serene and tranquil, the family hearth undisturbed. His gracious helpmeet had invited into the house a well dressed gentleman who asked to make a private business call to her husband.65

In a similar manner, Electrical Review tells a story of identity theft.

A clerk at the Ebbitt House, a leading society hotel in Washington, D.C., was deceived . . . . The clerk received a call from someone who claimed to be “Major Peabody, a well-known permanent guest of the house, who instructed the clerk to pay charges of $54.50 on a suit of clothes . . . . The fraud was discovered when the real Major Peabody arrived for dinner.66

The creation of new social roles was, in some cases, also a source of confusion. The electrical professional, the female telephone operator, and the telephone girl, for example, were new social roles. The telephone girl was a particular source of confusion and consternation to wives. Marvin explains that telephone girls were “domestic servants” that were “not within the household” and “quite outside its rules.”67 Marvin illustrates with a story from Telephony.

The predictable problems arose. Jones, a Boston businessman, had solicited the services of the “sweet-voiced” operator while his wife was on summer vacation. Mrs. Jones returned suddenly, and had a surprise waiting.

The next morning the telephone rings. Horrified hubby sits up in bed in dazed surprise, while Mrs. Jones goes to the ‘phone. “Hello,” says Mrs. Jones. “Hello, pet,” comes back in a woman’s voice. “Hurry up and get up. I’ve been ringing you long enough.”68

Social Adjustment

Although the telephone was the cause of some confusion, over time, society adapted and became acclimatized to the telephone. A new set of practices, expectations, rules, and understandings were developed in order to govern telephone interaction. For instance, the development of social expectations, such as when it was appropriate to call, helped maintain boundaries. Answering machines, secretaries, servants, and unlisted numbers were also used to maintain boundaries and to limit social access.69 In addition, people learned not to trust strangers, and not to disclose confidential information on the party line. Diplomats learned to adapt to the high speed of communication. People learned to understand and accept new social roles. And, the courts clarified how to apply the law in new situations.

The initial confusion caused by the introduction of the telephone did not have a lasting impact, in and of itself. Yet, it allowed for some social wiggle-room in which social norms and practices could, to some degree, as they were extended to new situations, be challenged and negotiated. In addition, the telephone made it more difficult to maintain social boundaries. It became more difficult, for instance, for a father to control his daughter’s access to young men of questionable reputation.

Conclusion

In order to properly understand the social impact of the telephone, it is important to appreciate that the impact of the telephone was both subtle and conspicuous, gradual and revolutionary. Ithiel de Sola Pool explains that the telephone has had multiple effects, often in opposite directions, “the telephone is an agent of action in many directions . . . its impacts are puzzling, evasive, and hard to pin down.”70 This is undoubtedly true. Nevertheless, the telephone has also had a number of effects that are relatively obvious.

The telephone was a new form of social contact, which was used to maintain personal relationships, to take care of daily business, and to provide entertainment. Although the telephone is used to maintain social contact, it is not ideal for maintaining long-term, long-distance relationships.

With the adoption of the telephone, there was a period of initial confusion, which required social adjustment. New customs and social expectations were developed to deal with new situations created by the telephone. Although there was some wiggle-room for the negotiation of social practices and norms, new rules and practices appear to be primarily an extension of old rules and practices. During the initial period of confusion, visionaries saw the potential of the new technology to redefine social interaction, and as seen in the literature of the time, expressed both their optimism and concern. The electrical journals, in particular, are filled with the utopian rhetoric of science and progress.

The telephone also changed business, and with business, daily life. The telephone altered the city’s skyline (the skyscraper and the suburb), changed the organization of business, and transformed global trade. It created new goods and services, changed daily business activities, and led to the creation of a new technical class or subclass.
