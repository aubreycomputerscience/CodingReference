AI as a Search Engine — Digital Services Act

  • ChatGPT must assess whether it contributes to illegal content, disinformation, election interference, discrimination, harms to minors, public-safety risks, or serious health and well-being harms.
  • It must take reasonable steps to reduce those risks, including changing how the service or its algorithms work if necessary.
  • Regulators can demand audits, data, and explanations of its systems.
  • Non-compliance can result in fines of up to 6% of worldwide annual revenue.

AI as an AI System — AI Act

  • OpenAI must provide documentation and transparency about how its AI systems are built and used.
  • More powerful models face requirements for risk testing, safety controls, cybersecurity, and incident reporting.
  • Some AI-generated content must be identified or labeled as AI-generated.

AI Using Personal Data — GDPR

  • OpenAI needs a lawful basis to collect and use personal data.
  • Users may have rights to access, correct, delete, or object to the use of their data.
  • OpenAI can face penalties if it improperly collects, stores, or exposes personal information.

AI Using Copyrighted Material — Copyright Law

  • Copyright law can apply to both training material and generated output.
  • OpenAI must have policies for complying with EU copyright law.
  • AI does not get a general exemption from copyright simply because the copying or generation is automated.

AI Causing Harm — Ordinary Liability Law

  • Harmful or false answers can still trigger laws on defamation, fraud, consumer protection, or negligence.
  • OpenAI is not automatically liable for every bad answer.
  • Liability depends on the type of harm, what OpenAI knew or should have known, and the law of the country involved.

What This Means in Practice

  • AI becomes harder and more expensive to build at scale. Large systems need legal review, audits, risk assessments, documentation, monitoring, and compliance staff.
  • The “common good” becomes a design constraint. AI systems may have to change behavior to reduce risks involving elections, minors, discrimination, public safety, health, and similar concerns.
  • Government helps define what counts as harm. Engineers and users are no longer the only ones deciding what the system should optimize for.
  • This favors large companies. Big firms can absorb compliance costs more easily than startups.
  • AI companies have stronger incentives to be cautious. Regulatory risk can push them toward more filtering, refusals, and conservative behavior.
  • The hardest issue is who defines harm. Illegal content is relatively clear; concepts such as disinformation, civic harm, or public well-being involve political and social judgment.

Bottom Line

The EU is treating powerful AI less like ordinary software and more like infrastructure with public consequences.

The design goal becomes not simply “build the best product,” but “build the best product within a legally defined view of acceptable social risk.”
