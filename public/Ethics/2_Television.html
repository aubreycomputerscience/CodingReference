The Television

Excerpts from Power, Madness, and Immortality: The Future of Virtual Reality (2005), Chapter 4

Introduction

Much of daily life has become organized around the television, and it has become a centerpiece of American domestic life. In fact, according to USA Today, Americans have more televisions per capita than bathrooms.1 The Ann Arbor News reports that high school students spend twice as much time watching television as they do in school.2

When we talk about the television, in contrast to the way we talk about the telephone, we have a tendency to talk about what it does, rather than what we use it for. This gives an insight into the nature of the television. The television is not a tool that we use to influence the world, but a tool used to influence us. The television teaches us about the world, and it changes the way we learn about the world. Newton Minow asserts:

It used to be said there were three great influences on a child: home, school, and church. Today there is a fourth great influence [television] . . . .3

. . .

The television is not only important for what it has given, but what it has taken away. The television, in particular, has led, or helped lead, to the decline of some practices and values. “In the good old days,” my grandmother admonishes, “the family used to eat dinner together, to talk to each other, read to each other. People used to sit on the front porch and talk to the neighbors as they walked by.” In addition, seniors complain that the television played a major role in the “erosion of morality.” In particular, they argue that the television played an important part in the breakdown of traditional Christian sexual norms.

. . .

It is also important to note that the television is part of, and contributes to, the process of modernization: nationalization, globalization, urbanization, consumerization, industrialization, post-industrialization, and related changes. Kottak reports that television is a significant part of a general process of urbanization, nationalization, and state solidification,5 and that village studies confirm that television can “spur participation in large-scale cultural and economic systems” and shift “loyalties from local to national events.”6

A Theater

The television is the theater of the masses, creating shared experiences and common understandings. It is the “electronic amphitheater.” Foucault describes the amphitheater of antiquity:

Antiquity had been a civilization of spectacle. “To render accessible to a multitude of men the inspection of a small number of objects”: this was the problem to which the architecture of temples, theatres and circuses responded . . . .19

The television is a national stage. It exposes us to common symbols, meanings, histories, and ideas. The television spreads new words and popularizes the names of television characters, as reflected in the popularity of these names for children.20

Images, Desire, and Revolution

Sukarno, the first president of Indonesia, a revolutionary and a nationalist, describes the power of the moving image:

The motion picture industry has provided a window on the world, and the colonized nations have looked through that window and have seen the things of which they have been deprived. It is perhaps not generally realized that a refrigerator can be a revolutionary symbol—to a people who have no refrigerators. A motor car owned by a worker in one country can be a symbol of revolt to a people deprived of even the necessities of life . . .

[Hollywood] helped to build up the sense of deprivation of man’s birthright, and that sense of deprivation has played a large part in the national revolutions of postwar Asia.35

Interpersonal Interaction

The television models interpersonal interaction, providing examples of how people interact with one another. After all, we often behave like the people we spend time with. It is not uncommon for children, in particular, to imitate behavior seen on television. Adults are also likely to imitate and experiment with behavior seen on television, but in a more subtle way. People “try out” different roles and strategies. They may try acting tough, being cool, or being sexy. The most obvious example of this is seen in the flirtatious behavior of teenage girls. Teenage girls learn how to dress, how to carry themselves, and how to address others, in part, from watching television.

In a similar manner, people from rural villages often learn how to interact with people from the city by watching television. Kottak tells a humorous story about an interview with a Brazilian woman from a rural town who had had little or no exposure to television. The interview was not altogether successful:

. . . her life experience provided absolutely no clue about why someone like me would want to talk to her for more than a minute—particularly to elicit her opinions . . . Sonia displayed the obsequious, eye-averting, voice-lowering, limp-handed demeanour that is often adopted by poor rural Bahians when they deal with perceived superiors.23

Several months later, however, Kottak interviewed her again, only to find that she interacted in what he perceived to be a more “normal fashion.” Sonia learned how to interact in this manner, according to her neighbors, from a recently purchased black-and-white television.

The importance of modeling appropriate interaction and behavior was a topic of conversation in China in 500 B.C. Confucius argued that, by providing proper models of human interaction and behavior, social stability could be achieved and maintained. Confucius created new definitions of words like “father,” which modeled or exemplified patterns of interaction and behavior. Smith explains:

Confucius says there is nothing more important than a father be a father, a ruler be a ruler, etc.—we must know what these words mean, and they must mean the right thing.24

In other words, Confucius suggests that one’s understanding of social interaction influences social interaction. His idealized definitions of various social roles, such as the father, have been woven into the fabric of Chinese culture and into almost every aspect of daily life. These definitions, or models of social interaction, to a large extent define social interaction in most Asian cultures to this day. If the television is able to model interpersonal interaction in a similar way, it may lead to a number of interesting social and cultural developments.

Does Television Make Us Stupid?

Critics have also suggested that television is making us “stupid.” Neil Postman, for instance, argues that television replaces “linear thought” with “pleasurable distractions”—“sensational,” “fragmented,” and “impersonal”—thus damaging our ability to think conceptually and logically:39

. . . the content of politics, religion, education, and anything else that comprises public business must change and be recast in terms that are most suitable to television.40

. . . turning everything into Las Vegas . . . .41

As evidence for this view, Postman continues, citing Robert MacNeil:

. . . Robert MacNeil, executive editor and co-anchor of the MacNeil-Lehrer NewsHour, writes . . . the idea [with regard to television news] . . . “is to keep everything brief, not to strain the attention of anyone but instead to provide constant stimulation through variety, novelty, action, and movement.”

. . . bite-sized is best, that complexity must be avoided, that nuances are dispensable, that qualifications impede the simple message, that visual stimulation is a substitute for thought, and that verbal precision is an anachronism.42

One may also note that “dead air” is a television anathema. In a similar manner, Nancee Cline suggests that Sesame Street changed the pace of daily life: children became addicted to stimulation; they have no patience, no concentration, no time for reflection, no creativity, and no quiet, empty space. Children expect to be entertained, making school boring and irrelevant.43

Psychologists have been particularly concerned about the influence of television violence. It is generally believed that watching violence on television increases the probability of subsequent violent behavior. Condry reports:

The question of a correlation between viewing violent television and acting violently is “not much in dispute”; the vast preponderance of evidence . . . clearly points to a substantial positive correlation.44

Television Violence

Nevertheless, it may be argued that a causal connection between violence on television and antisocial behavior has not been demonstrated, that violent programming is likely to influence only those prone to violence, or that this correlation merely reflects the fact that people with violent tendencies like to watch violent television.

That being said, there is a large body of psychological research that suggests that television violence does promote antisocial thought patterns and behaviors. Berkowitz explains that, “Not only does violent media play a role in encoding aggressive scripts, it also plays a central role in the maintenance of aggressive thoughts and ideas.”45 A number of experiments show a short-term connection between violent programming and behavior.

In a famous experiment, Liebert and Baron looked at the short-term influence of exposure to violent programming on children.46 First, Liebert and Baron showed children a violent clip from the television series The Untouchables. Then, the children were “told” that they could press a button to “help” or “hurt” another child. The study found that children exposed to the aggressive material were more willing to hurt another child.

In another famous experiment, Berkowitz and Rawlings found that television may amplify negative feelings and antisocial behaviors.47 The study found, not surprisingly, that a group of angry students exposed to violent material were more aggressive than angry students not exposed to the violent programming. Secondly, the study found that the angry students exposed to the material were more aggressive than students who were exposed to the material but were not angry.

Condry explains that violent material, in different situations, may promote or reduce aggression:

Exciting material may “unwind” or relax a person who is already excited about something else, but it may make this person even more excited if the story viewed is about the same thing that led to the original arousal. When television does “unwind” a person, when it undermines arousal, it does so because it disrupts the rehearsal process—thinking about the boss and why he or she made you mad—that tends to keep arousal high.48
