The Internet

Excerpt from Power, Madness, and Immortality: The Future of Virtual Reality (2005), Chapter 5

Introduction

The internet, for the most part, may be viewed as a combination of broadcast and interpersonal communication technologies, combining features of both the telephone and the television.

Like the television, the internet has the power to broadcast information, create shared meanings, teach us about the world, and entertain. Like the telephone, the internet increases the ease of communication and access to information, creates new efficiencies in business, and is used for social interaction. The internet ritualizes contact in new ways through netiquette, facilitates long-distance relationships, and provides new opportunities for individuals to “role-play.”

In addition to features of the telephone and television, the internet has several new features of its own. Virtual communities, such as chat rooms, are generally characterized by a new type of social interaction: small-group interaction. Second, the existence of virtual objects gives rise to a new type of consumer behavior, “virtual consumption”: the consumption of virtual objects and services. Third, it is not uncommon for users to engage in a kind of “nesting behavior.” In other words, users may modify virtual environments, whether a webpage, chat room, or internet-based virtual world, to make them feel a little more like home.

There is an interesting interplay between the broadcast and interpersonal features of the internet. Broadcast features emphasize control and the distribution of content, whereas interpersonal features challenge traditional broadcast structures, emphasizing diversity, fragmentation, and plurality. The interplay between these broadcast and interpersonal features is particularly important in the development and maintenance of virtual communities, in which social practices and norms are negotiated and defended.
