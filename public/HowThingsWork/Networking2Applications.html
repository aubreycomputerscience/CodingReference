Applications on the Internet

After computers are connected into networks, they can communicate with one another. Many connected networks form the internet, a worldwide network of interconnected computers. Once connected, computers use different applications and services to communicate, share information, and work together.

We can describe the primary ways people use the internet as applications. Each one is a distinct use case:

  • Web browsing: View webpages and other online content in a web browser.
  • Email: Send and receive electronic messages.
  • Cloud storage: Store, sync, and share files online.
  • Streaming: Play audio and video as it is delivered over the network.
  • Online games: Exchange real-time information between players and game servers.

The World Wide Web

The World Wide Web, or simply the Web, is one of the most common applications on the internet.

A Simple Analogy

Imagine you have a document you want to share with anyone who asks. Instead of mailing copies to everyone, you keep the document at your house.

When someone wants a copy, they send you a short request asking for it. You get the request, place a copy in an envelope, and mail it back. Anyone else who wants the document can do the same.

How the Web Works

The Web works in much the same way.

Instead of your house, the document lives on a web server—a computer that stores webpages and sends them when requested.

Instead of a street address, the server has an IP address. Since people generally use names such as google.com rather than remembering numerical addresses, the Domain Name System (DNS) looks up the IP address associated with the name.

Your web browser sends a request across the internet to the web server. The server receives that request and sends the webpage back. The browser then displays the page on your computer.

In reality, both the request and the webpage travel across the network in many small pieces called packets. Your computer receives those packets and puts the information back together.

From the user's point of view, this entire round trip usually happens almost instantly.

Email

Email is an internet application for sending and receiving electronic messages.

A Simple Analogy

Imagine sending a letter. You write it, put it in an envelope, address it, and drop it in a mailbox.

The postal service carries it to your friend's local post office and eventually to their mailbox. Your friend does not need to be home when the letter arrives. It can simply wait in the mailbox until they check for it.

How Email Works

Email works in much the same way.

Instead of a paper letter, you write an email message. Instead of a mailing address, you use an email address, such as alex@example.com.

When you click Send, your email program sends the message to an email server. That server stores it, finds the recipient's mail server, and transfers the message across the internet.

If the recipient is offline, the message simply waits on their mail server. When they open their email application, the message appears, ready to read.

Unlike the Web, where you usually expect an immediate response to a request, email is designed to be sent, stored, and delivered whenever the recipient is available.

Email and the Web both use the internet, but they are designed for different purposes. The Web is designed to retrieve information when you ask for it, while email is designed to send, store, and deliver messages between people.

Cloud Storage

Cloud storage lets you keep files on computers connected to the internet so you can reach them from almost anywhere.

A Simple Analogy

Imagine renting a storage locker. You put your things there so you can retrieve them later, and you can even let other people access them if you give them permission.

How Cloud Storage Works

Cloud storage works in a similar way. You upload files to cloud servers. Later, you can open or download them from another device.

Cloud storage can also synchronize files. If you change a file on one device, the service can update the stored copy so that the latest version is available on your other devices.

You can also give other people permission to access particular files or folders.

Unlike the Web or email, cloud storage is primarily designed to store, synchronize, and share files across devices.

Streaming

Streaming lets you watch video or listen to audio without waiting for the entire file to download first.

A Simple Analogy

Imagine drinking from a faucet. You do not wait for all the water in the pipes to arrive before you begin drinking. You use the water as it flows while more continues to arrive.

How Streaming Works

Streaming works in much the same way.

Your device receives a continuous flow of small pieces of audio or video data over the internet. As enough data arrives, your device begins playing it while additional data continues to arrive in the background.

If the flow slows down or stops, playback may pause until more data arrives. Streaming applications often keep a small amount of data waiting ahead of what you are currently watching or hearing. This is called buffering.

Unlike cloud storage, which is designed primarily to save files for later, streaming is designed to deliver audio and video continuously as you watch or listen.

Online Games

Online games use the internet to exchange information between players and game servers while a game is being played.

In an online game, your computer or game console continually sends information describing what you are doing. If you move a character, kick a ball, or place an object, that information must be communicated to the other computers involved in the game.

At the same time, your device receives information about what the other players are doing.

For many games, a game server acts as a central meeting point. Players send information to the server, the server keeps track of the current state of the game, and updated information is sent back to the players.

The important concern is often not the amount of information being transferred, but how quickly it arrives. A noticeable delay between an action and its result is called latency, or more informally, lag.

Online games are therefore designed for the rapid, continuous exchange of information between computers in real time.

Other Applications

Some applications work behind the scenes to help internet communication. People rarely notice them, but they provide services that other applications rely on.

DNS

The Domain Name System (DNS) helps computers find each other by translating names into IP addresses. It is a little like looking up someone's mailing address before sending a letter.

For example, people can remember a name such as google.com much more easily than a numerical IP address. DNS provides the lookup that connects the name to the address the computers use.

DHCP

Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol (DHCP) automatically gives a device the network information it needs when it connects.

You can think of it somewhat like checking into a hotel and having the front desk assign you a room. When your computer joins a network, DHCP can assign it an IP address and provide other information it needs to communicate on that network.

Neither DNS nor DHCP is normally something a person thinks about using directly. Instead, they work behind the scenes to make other network applications possible.

One Network, Different Applications

All of these applications use the same underlying internet, but they use it in different ways.

The Web retrieves information when it is requested. Email sends and stores messages for later retrieval. Cloud storage keeps files available and synchronized across devices. Streaming delivers media continuously as it is consumed. Online games exchange information rapidly enough for computers and players to interact in real time. DNS and DHCP provide supporting services that help these other applications communicate.

The network provides the connection. The application determines what that connection is used for and how information is exchanged.
