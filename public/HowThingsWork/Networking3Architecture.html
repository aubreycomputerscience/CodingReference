Network Architecture

Once computers are connected, there is another question to answer: How should the computers divide the work?

There are several ways to organize computers on a network. Two of the most important are peer-to-peer and client-server architecture.

Peer-to-Peer Architecture

The simplest arrangement is for two computers to communicate directly with one another. In a peer-to-peer system, the computers are essentially equals. Either computer can request information from the other, and either computer can provide information to the other. 

Computer A  ⇄  Computer B

As more computers join, each computer can potentially communicate directly with the others. 

Computer A  ⇄  Computer B
     ⇅              ⇅
Computer C  ⇄  Computer D

There does not have to be one central computer controlling the interaction. Each computer can provide its own information or resources directly to other computers. This approach can be simple and powerful, particularly when users want to communicate or share resources directly. However, it becomes more difficult when many people need to work with the same information or depend on the same services.

That leads to another architecture: client-server.

Client-Server Architecture

In a peer-to-peer system, computers communicate directly with one another. Either computer can request information, and either computer can provide it. Client-server architecture organizes things differently. Some computers are designated as servers and provide information or services to other computers. The computers that use those services are called clients.

For example, when you visit a website, your computer is the client. It asks a web server for a webpage, and the server sends the page back. Hundreds or thousands of other clients may be making requests to that same server at the same time. 

Client ──┐
Client ──┤
Client ──┼── Server
Client ──┤
Client ──┘

The important difference is that the computers now have different roles. Clients generally request and use services; servers are specifically set up to provide those services.

A Common Place for Data

One of the most important advantages of client-server architecture is that it provides a common place to keep information. Imagine a company with hundreds of employees and thousands of customers. The company needs one reliable set of customer records. If every employee maintained a separate copy, those copies would quickly become inconsistent.

Instead, the company can maintain a central database on its servers. Employees use client applications to view or change information in that database. 

Employee ──┐
Employee ──┤
Employee ──┼── Server ── Database
Employee ──┤
Employee ──┘

Everyone is therefore working with the same underlying information.

Centralized Services and Control

Servers can centralize more than data. They can provide applications, store files, manage user accounts, process transactions, control access to information, and perform computational work for clients. This is particularly useful for organizations. A company can maintain its important systems centrally rather than depending upon hundreds or thousands of individual computers to maintain separate copies.

Centralization also makes it easier to control who can access information, maintain backups, update systems, enforce organizational rules, and protect important data.

Why Client-Server Architecture Matters

Client-server architecture therefore provides two major benefits. First, it organizes computing around specialized roles. Clients request services, while servers are designed to provide them. Second, it brings shared information and services together. Data that needs to be shared can have a common home rather than being scattered among many independent computers.

This basic architecture appears throughout modern computing. Websites, email systems, business databases, cloud storage, social networks, banking systems, and countless other applications use some form of client-server architecture.

The basic idea is simple: Clients ask. Servers provide. And when many people need to work with the same information, the server can provide a common source of data and services for everyone.

A Brief History: Centralization and Decentralization

The story of computing is not just a list of technologies. Throughout its history, different ideas about where computing power and information should reside have helped shape the systems we use.

Mainframes and Dumb Terminals

Early organizational computing was dominated by mainframes: large, expensive computers that performed the actual computation for many users. Individual users were often given dumb terminals. A dumb terminal might provide little more than a keyboard and screen. It allowed someone to interact with the mainframe, but the terminal itself had very little computing power.

This arrangement was partly a consequence of the technology of the time, but it also fit the way large organizations operated. Computing resources were expensive and centrally administered, and system administrators generally did not want individual users modifying or controlling the underlying computer. Users were given access to the system, but not control over it. The intelligence, the data, and the control remained in the center.

The Personal Computer

The arrival of the personal computer challenged that model. Instead of giving a user a dumb terminal connected to somebody else's computer, the personal computer put a complete computer on the user's desk. That was an enormous change.

People could install software, write programs, store their own information, experiment, and use the computer in ways that a central administrator had never anticipated. Computing was no longer something that happened only inside a centralized system controlled by an organization. Personal computers gave individual users considerably more independence and control, and that freedom contributed to an explosion of new software and new uses for computers.

Client-Server Computing

But decentralization created its own problems. Businesses still needed employees to share information. A company could not reasonably maintain hundreds of unrelated versions of its customer database, accounting records, inventory, or other important information.

Client-server computing provided a way to combine the power of personal computers with centralized resources. The user's personal computer became the client. It could run its own software and perform work locally. But when it needed shared information or services, it could request them from a server.

The result was neither the old dumb-terminal model nor completely independent personal computers. Users had capable machines of their own, while organizations could still maintain common databases and shared services on central servers. Client-server architecture became a foundation of business computing and, eventually, of much of the internet.

Cloud Computing

As internet connections became faster and more reliable, servers no longer had to sit inside the same building—or even belong directly to the organization using them. Increasingly, computing and storage moved into enormous collections of servers housed in data centers. This became known as cloud computing.

From the user's perspective, a laptop or phone could still act as the client. But the server might now be hundreds or thousands of miles away, serving enormous numbers of users. This architecture also fit a powerful new business model: Software as a Service (SaaS).

Instead of selling someone a copy of a program that would run indefinitely on that person's computer, companies could operate the software on their own servers and charge customers for continuing access—often through a monthly or yearly subscription. Cloud computing therefore reflects both technological developments and economic choices. Centralized servers make it possible to aggregate enormous amounts of data and computing power, but they also give the organizations operating those servers considerable control over the software, the data, and the relationship with the customer.

Decentralized Systems

The movement toward centralization has never been the only approach. Peer-to-peer systems allow computers to communicate and share resources more directly rather than requiring everything to pass through one central service.

More recently, blockchains and cryptocurrencies have explored another form of decentralization. Rather than relying on one organization to maintain the authoritative record, a blockchain distributes that record across many computers. These technologies reflect a very different view of how computer systems can be organized.

What Comes Next?

AI may be the next chapter in this story. The largest AI systems depend heavily on enormous centralized data centers. At the same time, increasingly capable AI models can run directly on personal computers, phones, and other local devices.

That creates an interesting question for the next generation of computing: How much computation and data will remain centralized, and how much will move back onto the devices people control themselves? The answer is still being written.
